Major Impact Project, 2026

Passing the Torch

This study examines how Indian family enterprises can professionalise their management during generational succession, and how a successor can modernise a founder-led business without eroding the trust and legacy on which it was built.

Executive summary

Successors and owners measure credibility differently

Drawing on a survey of the next generation of business families and case studies of three owner-led enterprises, this project tests the assumptions behind my earlier Legacy Renewal Framework and translates them into a practical onboarding framework for successors.

The credibility gap

Next-generation respondents lean towards running operations well as the way to earn trust. Owners look further ahead: they judge a successor by the growth they can deliver.

Knowledge stays with the owner

Roles and routines are largely “understood and guided” rather than written down, yet successors rarely treat capturing that knowledge as a priority.

Expertise is bought, control is kept

The owners studied will bring in engineers, lawyers and other specialists, but decision-making authority stays firmly within the family.

The challenge

When the founder is the system

Family enterprises are central to Indian business, and diversified business groups, many of them family-controlled, play an outsized role in the economy (Khanna & Palepu, 2000). In many of these firms, every consequential decision still runs through the founder. The arrangement can work well for decades, until the next generation arrives.

Centralised authority

Approvals, pricing and hiring rest with a single individual, so the enterprise slows the moment that person steps back.

Overlapping roles

Family titles and job titles blur. When family and business overlap this closely, the same ties that build loyalty can also turn commercial disagreements personal (Tagiuri & Davis, 1996).

Change read as criticism

When a successor introduces new software or processes, it can be heard as a verdict on how the business has always been run.

This project builds directly on my first paper, Balancing Legacy and Modernisation (IOSR Journal of Business and Management, 2025), which synthesised 52 sources into the Legacy Renewal Framework. That paper asked what succession requires in theory. This project asks what a successor should actually do in their first months.
Research design

A mixed-methods approach

The study pairs breadth with depth: a survey captures how the next generation views succession, while case studies show how owners themselves experience it. Fieldwork was conducted in October 2026.

Quantitative

Next-generation survey

Students and young adults from business-owning families completed a structured questionnaire, shared through school networks and LinkedIn. It examined how their family enterprises are governed, where friction arises and what they believe earns a successor trust. Each participant could respond once, and results are reported only in aggregate.

Qualitative

Owner case studies

I held structured conversations with the owners of three enterprises, ranging from a small local business to an export-oriented manufacturer. Each covered succession planning, decision-making, openness to change and the use of outside professionals. One of the three owners is a member of my family.

Foundation

Literature synthesis

The Legacy Renewal Framework from my first paper, which draws on 52 sources on succession, governance, knowledge transfer and strategic renewal, supplied the hypotheses tested here.

Field context

Practitioner exposure

The research question first took shape during internships with a residential real-estate developer (2025) and a jewellery retailer (2026) in Siliguri.

Scope. The study is exploratory by design. With a modest survey sample and three cases, it surfaces patterns and tensions that merit deeper investigation rather than claiming to represent Indian family businesses as a whole.

Key findings

What the evidence suggests

Three hypotheses from the literature were tested against the survey and the case studies. One was partly supported, one was supported with a qualification and one was refined.

1
Hypothesis 1: partly supported

Successors and owners define credibility differently

I expected successors to earn trust fastest by fixing day-to-day operations before proposing strategic change. Next-generation respondents broadly agreed: more of them chose operational reliability than bold strategy, although the most common view was that it depends on the family and the business.

The owners looked further ahead. Every owner named growth, whether new markets or new projects, as a mark of a capable successor, and most paired it with keeping the core business running well. This credibility gap is the clearest pattern in the study. A successor who focuses only on operations may be solving a problem the owner does not consider the priority.

2
Hypothesis 2: supported, with a qualification

Critical knowledge remains tacit

Apart from the exporter, whose international operations demand written systems, the case enterprises run on unwritten roles that are, in one owner’s words, “understood and guided”. Yet not a single survey respondent chose documenting roles and processes as their first change.

The knowledge that most needs capturing is precisely the knowledge no one prioritises capturing. This is a classic tacit-knowledge problem (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995), and research identifies the transfer of such knowledge from predecessor to successor as decisive in succession (Cabrera-Suárez et al., 2001).

“I wished I knew the depth of my business from the very beginning…”

Survey respondent
3
Hypothesis 3: refined

The friction is relational, not structural

I expected unclear decision rights to be the main source of conflict. Respondents rarely cited them. Instead, they pointed to the adoption of new technology, the older generation’s reluctance to change and family disagreements spilling into the business.

The more effective lever may therefore be separating family conversations from business decisions. One case enterprise already draws this line: ownership stays within the family, while day-to-day management can pass to a salaried manager. This is consistent with research showing that relationship conflict erodes family-firm performance, while productive family relationships strengthen it (Eddleston & Kellermanns, 2007).

“…how deep in personal life professional family decisions become.”

Survey respondent, on what they have learnt
Case studies

Three enterprises, one pattern

To look beneath the survey results, I studied three owner-led enterprises as illustrative cases. They range in size and sector, from a small local business to an export-oriented manufacturer, yet they share a common pattern. Owners are not named, to protect their privacy.

What the three enterprises share

The owner remains the final authority

In every case, consequential decisions rest with the owner, informed by family members, advisers or senior staff. None described a formal succession plan.

Change must earn its place

New ideas from the younger generation are welcomed when they are compelling, but setbacks leave a mark. In one case, ventures that failed have made the family more cautious.

Expertise is hired, control is retained

All three use, or are open to using, outside professionals for capabilities the family lacks, yet control remains with the owner in every case. This is consistent with research on the priority family owners place on preserving control, part of what scholars call socioemotional wealth (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007).

Where they differ. Formalisation scales with complexity. Only the exporter runs on documented roles and systems, which suggests, in this small sample, that formal governance is adopted when a business outgrows informal trust, not before. Professionalisation is not a single switch; it takes different forms in different family firms (Stewart & Hitt, 2012).
Published cases point the same way. India’s largest family groups have faced the same tension between family control and professional management. At Dabur, the Burman family decided in 1998 that family members would no longer hold management roles, keeping ownership and board seats instead. At Marico, founder Harsh Mariwala chose an insider professional, Saugata Gupta, rather than his son to lead the company, and Gupta became managing director in 2014 (Sharma, 2025; Marico Limited, 2014). In 2024, the Godrej family divided its group by mutual agreement, with each side keeping the businesses it already ran (Kartik, 2024). In each case, the decisive step was a governance choice, not an operational one.
The output

The Next-Gen Onboarding Framework

The draft toolkit proposed three steps: audit operations, modernise non-core systems and formalise governance. The evidence reshaped it in two ways: owners expect a successor to deliver growth, not merely order, and they prefer to buy expertise rather than build it. The revised framework therefore has four steps.

Step 1, retained

Master and stabilise the operation

  • Map who decides what today
  • Keep daily operations running smoothly before changing them
  • Identify the two or three bottlenecks that cost the most time or money

Evidence: a well-run operation matters to successors and to most owners, even if owners expect more than that.

Step 2, new

Lead one growth initiative

  • Tie any new system to a concrete expansion: a new market, product line or channel
  • Pilot new tools alongside existing methods before switching
  • Report results in the measures the owner already uses

Evidence: owners judge a successor by the growth they deliver.

Step 3, new

Orchestrate outside expertise

  • Bring in engineers, lawyers or other specialists where the family lacks capability
  • Define their mandate and the limits of their authority at the outset
  • Act as the bridge between professionals and the family

Evidence: the case enterprises use, or are open to using, specialists while keeping control within the family.

Step 4, re-sequenced

Formalise as the business outgrows trust

  • Document roles, decision rights and approval limits once the firm begins exporting, appoints a non-family manager or welcomes the next generation
  • Hold regular family meetings, separate from business reviews

Evidence: formal governance appeared only where complexity demanded it. Relational and formal governance both improve strategic decisions in family firms (Mustakallio et al., 2002).

Implications

What this means in practice

Succession is best understood as a multi-stage process rather than a single handover (Le Breton-Miller et al., 2004). These recommendations focus on one stage in particular: a successor’s first months in the business.

For successors

  • Earn the right to change. Master the operation first; credibility built on reliability buys room for ambition.
  • Speak the language of growth. Present every new system as a route to expansion, not as a correction of the past.
  • Become the orchestrator. Your value lies less in knowing everything than in assembling and directing the right expertise.

For owners

  • Define success out loud. Tell your successor what a good first year looks like; the generations often assume different answers.
  • Capture what only you know. Record key relationships, pricing logic and decision rules before the transition, not after it.
  • Separate the dining table from the boardroom. Hold family meetings apart from business reviews, and keep personal tensions out of commercial decisions.
